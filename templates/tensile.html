Working Principle & Function

Consists of an elastic metallic proof body bonded with foil strain gauges in a full Wheatstone bridge configuration. Under applied tensile tension, minuscule elastic strain alters resistance, producing a proportional millivolt electrical signal calibrated directly to force.

Technical Specifications
Capacity: 50 kN | Accuracy: Class 0.5 (ISO 7500-1) | Non-linearity: ±0.25% Full Scale
Laboratory Operation & Safety
Never exceed rated load capacity (overload limit: 150%). Tare/Zero the load cell reading prior to gripping the specimen.

ASTM E8 / ISO 6892 Tensile Specimen Geometry

Accurate tensile property measurement requires standardized specimen geometry. The test piece comprises enlarged grip sections (ends) and a reduced central parallel section (gauge section).

Gauge Length ($L_0$)

The primary calibrated length over which strain and elongation are recorded. In proportional test pieces, $L_0 = 5 \times d_0$ (e.g. $L_0 = 50.0 \text{ mm}$ for $d_0 = 10.0 \text{ mm}$).

Shoulder Transition Fillet ($R$)

A generous smooth transition radius ($R \ge 10 \text{ mm}$) connects the gauge length to the larger grip ends to avoid notch stress concentrations.

Grip Ends ($D$)

Enlarged diameter ($D = 16 \text{ mm}$) to accommodate serrated wedge jaws, ensuring no plastic deformation occurs inside the grip fixtures.

Mechanical Metallurgy & Deformation Mechanics

When a ductile metallic specimen undergoes uniaxial tension, deformation progresses through distinct physical regimes governed by dislocation dynamics and crystal plasticity.

5 Progressive Stages of Tensile Ductile Fracture
1. Elastic Deformation (Hooke's Law)

At small loads, strain is completely reversible. The interatomic bonds stretch elastically without dislocation movement. Governed by Hooke's Law:
σ = E × ε
where $E$ is Young's Modulus (stiffness).

2. Yielding & Plastic Flow

At the yield strength ($\sigma_y$), shear stresses activate dislocation glide along slip planes. In mild steel, interstitial carbon atoms pin dislocations (Cottrell atmospheres), producing upper and lower yield points with Lüders band propagation.

3. Strain Hardening & UTS

Dislocations multiply and entangle, increasing resistance to further plastic deformation. The load reaches a global maximum ($P_{max}$) defining the Ultimate Tensile Strength (UTS). At UTS, $d\sigma / d\epsilon = \sigma$ (Considère's Criterion).

4. Localized Necking & Cup-and-Cone Rupture

Cross-sectional shrinkage concentrates at the weakest gauge section. Triaxial tensile stress induces central microvoid nucleation around second-phase inclusions, coalescing into an internal crack that terminates in 45° shear lips ('cup-and-cone').

Standard Operating Procedure (ASTM E8 / ISO 6892-1)

1
Specimen Dimension Inspection

Measure the initial gauge diameter ($d_0$) at three locations using a digital vernier micrometer (0.01 mm resolution). Record the mean diameter and calculate original area $A_0 = \pi d_0^2 / 4$.

2
Punch Gauge Marking

Using a center punch and divider gauge, lightly place two punch marks separated exactly by the standard gauge length ($L_0 = 50.0 \text{ mm}$) along the center of the reduced section.

3
Specimen Gripping & Alignment

Insert specimen ends into upper and lower hydraulic wedge grips. Verify vertical collinearity to avoid bending moments. Apply hydraulic clamping pressure (150–200 bar).

4
Extensometer Mounting & Taring

Attach the knife-edge averaging clip-on extensometer across the punch marks. Zero/Tare the load cell readout and extensometer channels in the DAQ console.

5
Quasi-Static Uniaxial Loading

Set crosshead displacement rate to standard quasi-static strain rate (0.5 to 2.0 mm/min). Initiate crosshead motion and log load-extension pairs at 50–100 Hz.

6
Necking, Extensometer Removal & Fracture

Observe neck formation. If using a delicate mechanical extensometer, remove it prior to rupture to prevent shock damage. Run machine until specimen fractures completely.

7
Post-Test Fracture Dimensional Analysis

Fit the two fractured specimen halves snugly together. Measure the final distance between gauge marks ($L_f$) and minimum neck diameter ($d_f$). Calculate % Elongation and % Reduction in Area.

Standard Mechanical Property Formulas

Original Cross-Sectional Area ($A_0$)
A₀ = (π × d₀²) / 4

Used as the nominal reference area for all engineering stress calculations.

Engineering Stress ($\sigma$)
σ = P / A₀ (N/mm² or MPa)

Ratio of instantaneous axial force to original undeformed cross-sectional area.

Engineering Strain ($\epsilon$)
ε = ΔL / L₀ = (L - L₀) / L₀

Linear elongation relative to original gauge length (dimensionless or %).

Young's Modulus ($E$)
E = Δσ / Δε (in GPa)

Slope of the linear elastic proportional limit region determined by linear regression.

0.2% Offset Proof Stress ($\sigma_{0.2\%}$)
σ = E × (ε - 0.002)

Intersection of offset linear slope with the stress-strain curve for materials lacking sharp yield points.

Percentage Elongation (%EL) & Reduction in Area (%RA)
%EL = [(L_f - L₀) / L₀] × 100%
%RA = [(A₀ - A_f) / A₀] × 100%

Primary engineering measures of metallurgical ductility.

Experimental Objective

Primary Goal: To determine the fundamental mechanical and elastic properties of standard engineering metallic specimens subjected to uniaxial tensile loading in accordance with ASTM E8 / ISO 6892-1 standards.

Specific Learning Outcomes:
  • Operate a simulated Universal Testing Machine (UTM) and identify all primary sensory and kinematic subcomponents.
  • Convert raw laboratory force-extension records into standard Engineering Stress-Strain curves.
  • Calculate Young's Modulus ($E$) using linear least-squares regression within the elastic regime.
  • Identify the Upper and Lower Yield Points or apply the 0.2% offset proof stress method for continuous yielding alloys.
  • Determine the Ultimate Tensile Strength (UTS), Modulus of Resilience ($U_r$), and Tensile Toughness ($U_t$).
  • Quantify ductility through percentage elongation (%EL) and percentage reduction in area (%RA).
  • Evaluate fracture surface morphology (cup-and-cone ductile shear vs flat cleavage rupture).
